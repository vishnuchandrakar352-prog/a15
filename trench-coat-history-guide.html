A Short History of the Trench Coat

Few garments have travelled as far as the trench coat: from muddy fields to city streets, film sets and almost every wardrobe.

Waterproof beginnings

In the nineteenth century, makers experimented with rubberised cotton and then with tightly woven, water-repellent gabardine. These fabrics kept out rain while still letting the body breathe, which made a light, practical overcoat possible.

Made for the trenches

During the First World War, officers needed a coat lighter than the heavy wool greatcoat. The shorter, belted, water-resistant coat that resulted was worn in the trenches, and the name stuck. Many of its details were designed for that life.

Details with a purpose

Look closely and the uniform origins are everywhere:

  • Epaulettes and a storm flap at the shoulder.
  • A throat latch to close the collar.
  • Cuff straps and a belt with metal rings.

From uniform to icon

After the war, returning officers kept wearing their coats, and civilians adopted them. Film stars made the belted trench a symbol of mystery and style, and by mid-century it was worn by men and women alike.

The trench today

Modern trench coats come in every length and colour, single or double-breasted, in cotton, wool and technical fabrics. The classic version remains a knee-length, double-breasted, belted coat in a stone or khaki shade.
